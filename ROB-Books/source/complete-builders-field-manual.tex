\section{Design principles that recur}

Across the sources, several durable principles emerge:

\begin{itemize}
  \item use prototypes to discover packaging and load problems;
  \item separate high-level intent from low-level actuator values;
  \item keep independent stop layers close to each source of failure;
  \item isolate optional cameras, Python helpers, video, speech, and AI from core motion control;
  \item preserve service access and label wiring;
  \item make unsupported capability fail honestly;
  \item collect evidence before calling a proposal implemented.
\end{itemize}

\section{Physical and information paths}

The robot contains two overlapping architectures:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \textbf{energy architecture} moves stored electrical energy through protection, conversion, controllers, conductors, and motors.
  \item The \textbf{information architecture} moves intent and measurements through interfaces, authentication, state machines, protocols, adapters, firmware, and feedback.
\end{enumerate}

The two paths meet at motor controllers and actuators. Keeping their documentation separate prevents a common error: assuming that a correct data value proves the high-current circuit is safe, or that a correctly wired motor proves the command path is authorized.

\section{Desired component ownership map}

The table below states the target responsibility split. It is a design contract, not a claim that every current implementation already satisfies every responsibility; gaps must remain visible in the subsystem status and verification chapters.

\midrule

\section{Sense-plan-act without surrendering authority}

\ROBSimpleSystemDiagram

Manual control, deterministic autonomy, and AI-assisted behavior can all use the same physical plant, but they should enter through explicit authority gates. \Experimental\quad Current social-roam autonomy consumes fresh filtered lidar scans and, near 82 percent of its configured activation radius, attempts to turn and drive toward its origin. That behavior is not a physical geofence and does not prove containment. Optional Gemini tools declare high-level action proposals, but most do not yet have executors. Neither path should bypass local validation, the control-master state, the Arduino deadline, or the physical emergency stop.

\begin{advancedbox}
Document safety as a control hierarchy, not a list of features. For every hazard, name the prevention, detection, mitigation, independent stop, diagnostic coverage, proof test, and residual risk. Ordinary cameras, IR sensors, and software timers may support risk reduction but are not automatically safety-rated.
\end{advancedbox}

The base is the foundation for every later system. \section{Start from interfaces}

Before drawing the final plate, freeze or explicitly version these interfaces:

\begin{itemize}
  \item tread-frame mounting points and full motion envelope;
  \item drive and idler shaft positions, diameters, retention, and bearing supports;
  \item motor face, shaft, and cable clearances;
  \item chain line, sprocket sizes, guard volume, and service access;
  \item flipper shaft, torque path, rear/front location, and end stops;
  \item battery envelope, restraint, protection access, and removal path;
  \item actuator mounts and full extension/retraction envelope;
  \item lifting, tie-down, stand, cover, and inspection points.
\end{itemize}

\ROBPhotoTwo{2022-drive-assembly-rear.jpg}{A centered rear view shows the drive assembly between two treads.}{2024-sprocket-machined-opening.jpg}{A later sprocket view shows why chain-line and plate clearance need dimensioned control.}

\section{Tread tension and support}

The presentation describes aluminum bars with threaded holes that pull the tread assembly outward, free-spinning wheels retained on shafts, and a heavy-load problem that led to improvised HDPE and skateboard-bearing supports. These are valuable design clues but not a final service specification.

Record a repeatable tension method. A statement such as ``tight enough'' cannot be audited. Depending on the tread system, the specification might be deflection under a known force, tensioner position, exposed thread length, torque plus verification, or the manufacturer's procedure.

\ROBPhotoTwo{2022-chain-sprocket-interior.jpg}{A large sprocket and chain are visible inside the base.}{2024-chain-drive-closeup.jpg}{The close chain path illustrates alignment, abrasion, and guarding concerns.}

\begin{safetybox}
Chains, sprockets, and treads create drawing-in, nip, shear, and entanglement hazards. Disconnect and verify power, block stored motion, prevent unintended rolling, and use fixed guards before hands enter the mechanism.
\end{safetybox}

\section{Flipper revision and third-wheel concept}

Presentation pages 21-26 say the flippers moved from the front wheels to the rear after a design revision. The deck also records a one-degree bar offset, cotter-pin concerns, and a proposed perpendicular third wheel raised by an actuator to reduce tread scrub while turning.

\ROBPhoto[0.54\textheight]{rob-v3-page-23.jpg}{The annotated upside-down prototype on presentation page 23 captures the third-wheel idea and identifies a design revision. It is not a final drawing.}

\Planned\quad Treat the third wheel as a separate design project until the builder supplies the geometry, load path, stability analysis, actuator selection, ground clearance, deployed/retracted sensing, failure response, and test evidence.

\section{Provisional assembly sequence to verify}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item inspect plate dimensions, datum features, threads, deburring, and finish;
  \item install bearings, shaft supports, and fixed structural brackets;
  \item trial-fit tread frames, drive motors, shafts, sprockets, and chains without power;
  \item measure shaft parallelism, sprocket coplanarity, tension range, and full-motion clearance;
  \item install flipper shaft and drivetrain with temporary manual limits;
  \item fit actuator brackets through the full stroke with the load supported independently;
  \item install guarded power components and labeled harnesses only after mechanical inspection;
  \item add batteries using a rated lift and restraint plan;
  \item perform continuity, insulation, polarity, and protective-device checks;
  \item test one actuator at a time on stands under a bounded current and command limit;
  \item install guards and covers before floor testing.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Build above the base}

The upper structure must hold cameras and arms while surviving acceleration, arm moments, cable pull, transport, and public interaction. The presentation openly records weak prototype areas: body-to-torso joints pulling on metal, bent L brackets, and wooden parts glued and screwed together.

\section{Body-to-torso connection}

Presentation pages 56-60 show the connection under load and call for mechanical improvement. A final design should provide:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a defined load path into the chassis;
  \item bearing surfaces or pivots sized for radial, axial, and moment loads;
  \item hard stops and motion limits independent of software;
  \item retention that remains safe after one fastener loosens, where practicable;
  \item inspection access and visible wear indicators;
  \item cable routing that does not become the motion stop;
  \item a transport lock that does not depend on powered holding torque.
\end{itemize}

\section{Body rotation and cable continuity}

Presentation page 67 proposes a 36 mm motor and a body bearing with rotation constrained from $-\pi$ to $+\pi$ instead of using a slip ring. A nearby restricted B-COMMAND file exists, but its contents are excluded and the firmware and photographs do not prove that the associated component is installed.

\Planned\quad A rotating body requires a controlled cable-wrap budget or an approved rotary interface, home/index sensing, hard and soft limits, collision envelopes, and a recovery plan after position loss.

\chapter{Fabrication and finish}

The presentation asks how to print and weld the aluminum, whether templates or a 3D scan would help, and whether an outside robotics shop should quote the metalwork. Those questions prove that a complete final fabrication recipe is not present. This chapter supplies the record structure the builder should fill.

\section{Measurement plan}

Choose datums before collecting dimensions. For every critical measurement, record:

\begin{itemize}
  \item feature and datum names;
  \item nominal value and unit;
  \item tolerance or functional clearance;
  \item instrument and resolution;
  \item repeated measurements and uncertainty;
  \item temperature or load state if relevant;
  \item photograph or drawing revision;
  \item who measured and when.
\end{itemize}

\ROBPhotoTwo{2024-caliper-tread-measurement.jpg}{A digital caliper is used across the tread frame; the publication needs the datum and recorded value.}{2022-bracket-fit-check.jpg}{A fit check can reveal interface errors before a hole becomes permanent.}

\section{Process traveler}

For each fabricated part, create a traveler with these gates:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item released drawing and material certificate or stock identification;
  \item saw/cut blank with allowance;
  \item workholding setup and datum pickup;
  \item roughing operation;
  \item intermediate inspection;
  \item finish holes, pockets, threads, bends, or weld preparation;
  \item deburr and edge-break requirement;
  \item weld/braze/bond sequence and distortion check, if applicable;
  \item surface preparation and coating;
  \item final dimensions, photographs, and acceptance signature;
  \item nonconformance and rework record.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{safetybox}
The builder or qualified shop must write the actual machine, cutter, feeds and speeds, workholding, coolant, chip control, guarding, ventilation, hot-work, welding, grinding, coating, and PPE instructions. A generic book cannot make a particular shop operation safe.
\end{safetybox}

\section{Design for assembly and service}

Use captive or accessible fasteners where covers need regular removal. Separate abrasive motion and sharp edges from harnesses. Provide inspection windows for chain line, tension, battery restraints, and protective devices. Ensure a heavy component can be supported before its final fastener is removed. Include lifting points and a stand plan in the CAD, not as an afterthought.

Cerebro consumes the existing \texttt{WARNING! FRONT}, \texttt{WARNING! BACK}, and blocking-error sentences without requiring a firmware flash. Its SceneKit diagnostic highlights the corresponding pair for three seconds. Silence becomes ``clearance unknown,'' not ``path clear,'' because the legacy stream has neither explicit clear events nor numeric distances. These terrain-sensitive IR warnings do not replace RPLidar traversability or a physical stop.

The existing Base sketch emits \texttt{BEGIN BASE STARTUP SEQUENCE} after IMU initialization. Cerebro pulses DTR to restart each USB serial candidate, waits up to 15 seconds for that exact role-specific line, retains the matching descriptor, and reruns the scan when the operator refreshes. It transmits no command bytes and requires no firmware flash. This removes reliance on a hub-dependent \texttt{/dev/cu.*} name, but it is role discovery rather than cryptographic authentication or hardware verification.

The 42 ASCII bytes represent left brake, left signed speed, right brake, right signed speed, flipper brake, flipper signed speed, and actuator signed speed. Brake field 0 maps to HIGH and is described as released; nonzero maps to LOW and is described as engaged. Tread and flipper speed output is \texttt{255 - abs(command)}, consistent with the active-low motor control concept. The physical brake electronics are not documented by the five-wire motor outline.

\section{Actuator compact serial}

The code sends 0x83 to exit safe start, 0x85 for forward, and 0x86 for reverse. It encodes magnitude in a five-bit low chunk and remaining high bits. Special input values +3201 and -3201 also clear safe start before becoming +3200 or -3200. Reset should be separated from speed in a future typed protocol.

\section{IR and IMU status}

\Experimental\quad Six IR sensors are constructed and front/back readings are sampled. After ten repeated close readings, the code prints a warning. The two lines that assert movement-inhibit flags are commented out, side sensors do not participate, and full six-value telemetry is commented. The builder reports grass/floor false triggers.\par

\Implemented\quad MPU9250 acceleration, gyro, and magnetometer readings feed a Mahony quaternion filter and print yaw, pitch, roll, and temperature. The orientation is telemetry only; tilt does not inhibit motion. The yaw correction is hard-coded for an older Austin, Texas declination and must not be treated as current calibration.

\section{Safety and correctness audit}

The historical firmware should be preserved as an artifact, then replaced behind fixtures. Priority defects include:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the five-byte numeric buffer lacks a C-string null terminator before \texttt{atoi} and debug printing;
  \item one available byte triggers 41 further reads without proof of a complete frame;
  \item separator positions, signs, ranges, checksum, and frame completeness are not validated;
  \item an extra read after the seventh field can consume the next frame's start marker;
  \item any received byte refreshes the deadman before frame validation;
  \item the deadman depends on loop counts rather than elapsed monotonic time;
  \item timeout sends zero speed with brake fields that release brakes;
  \item IR inhibit assignments are disabled and would not update outputs until another command path runs;
  \item one rotation test duplicates the same condition and omits the mirrored turn;
  \item command values are not clamped to PWM and actuator ranges;
  \item ramp constants, encoder feedback, and voltage monitoring are TODO-only;
  \item brake, direction, and PWM update ordering has not been proven jerk-free;
  \item the flipper and actuator have no firmware-visible end stops, position feedback, or motion timeout;
  \item setup writes the inactive value 255 to both tread PWM pins but never initializes flipper PWM D6; after \texttt{pinMode(OUTPUT)}, its default LOW is the maximum-command level under the active-low mapping, so boot safety relies on the unverified flipper-brake path;
  \item IMU initialization can abort before motor outputs are explicitly initialized safe;
  \item direction method names, comments, and debug strings disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{safetybox}
Do not ``clean up'' direction logic on the live robot by inspection. First capture protocol fixtures, lift and guard the drivetrain, limit current and speed, verify every brake and direction state, measure timeout behavior, and retain an immediate independent stop.
\end{safetybox}

\section{Replacement-firmware architecture}

A modern base controller should contain:

\begin{itemize}
  \item safe GPIO initialization before optional sensors;
  \item bounded ring buffer and explicit framed decoder;
  \item version, length, type, sequence, range, and checksum validation;
  \item atomic command acceptance only after a complete valid frame;
  \item a monotonic deadline refreshed only by a valid authorized command;
  \item explicit states: boot-safe, disarmed, armed-neutral, moving, stopping, fault, E-stop;
  \item separate commands for reset, arm, drive, flipper, actuator, and diagnostic requests;
  \item clamped outputs, ramp and direction-change interlocks, and defined brake/coast policy;
  \item end-stop, encoder, current, voltage, and temperature feedback where hardware supports it;
  \item structured telemetry with units and status flags;
  \item unit, parser-fuzz, timing, and hardware-in-the-loop tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scale into the encoder's pixel format}

\texttt{ROBCameraH264Encoder} creates a \texttt{VTCompressionSession} whose destination buffers use video-range bi-planar 4:2:0 YCbCr (NV12). A reusable \texttt{VTPixelTransferSession} scales the source with letterboxing so aspect ratio is preserved. The compression-session pool supplies destination buffers; allocation uses a threshold of six so pressure becomes a dropped frame rather than unbounded buffer growth.

At 20 fps, the target interval is 50 ms. Samples arriving sooner are intentionally skipped before conversion. A dropped allocation or compression result requests a future recovery key frame. Every completion object can be claimed once, preventing synchronous drop flags and an asynchronous VideoToolbox callback from delivering the same logical completion twice.

\section{Configure low-latency H.264}

The encoder requests real-time operation, disables frame reordering, prefers constrained baseline profile when supported, supplies expected frame rate and average bitrate, and limits the key-frame interval to approximately one second. Disabling reordering means this initial stream does not depend on B-frames; decode timestamps can remain invalid and presentation order follows the reliable stream.

Each encoded result owns copied bytes rather than a borrowed VideoToolbox buffer. The encoder records a monotonically increasing access-unit sequence, capture time in Unix milliseconds, presentation time and duration in microseconds, synchronization/key-frame state, NAL-length width, SPS/PPS parameter sets when present, and one complete AVCC access-unit payload.

\section{Understand AVCC, SPS, PPS, and access units}

H.264 consists of Network Abstraction Layer (NAL) units. Annex-B streams separate NAL units with start codes such as \texttt{00 00 00 01}. This implementation does not transmit Annex-B. AVCC stores a big-endian byte length before each NAL unit. The receiver must use the negotiated length-field width; it must not search for start-code patterns inside compressed data.

The Sequence Parameter Set (SPS) describes coded properties needed to construct a decoder format. The Picture Parameter Set (PPS) supplies picture-level decoding parameters. Cerebro sends them in a codec-configuration message with a positive generation before access units using that generation. A new configuration, encoder recovery, or requested key frame resends the configuration. An IDR key frame lets the decoder restart without depending on earlier reference pictures.

\begin{advancedbox}
Codec configuration is state, not decoration. A payload can be valid H.264 bytes yet undecodable with the wrong SPS/PPS, NAL-length width, dimensions, stream ID, or generation. Validate all of them before asking Core Media to allocate decoder state.
\end{advancedbox}

\section{Wrap media in two bounded layers}

The outer ordered-stream frame is \texttt{RVID} version 1. Its 32-byte header carries magic, version, header length, message kind, bounded payload length, monotonic connection sequence, and reserved-zero fields. Authentication, capability, subscription, feedback, configuration, access-unit, and end messages receive distinct kinds.

Encoded configuration and access units use an inner \texttt{RBVD} version-1 header of 92 bytes. It binds media to session UUID, subscription UUID, media sequence, capture time, presentation time, duration, timescale, codec-configuration generation, NAL-length width, flags, and payload length. Integers are network byte order. Current hard payload limits are 64 KiB for codec configuration and 2 MiB for one access unit; control JSON is capped at 64 KiB and rejects unknown fields.

\section{Apply backpressure before latency accumulates}

Video has its own listener, connections, queue, encoder, authentication domain, and send state. It never shares the control message queue. Cerebro permits only one pending raw source frame, one pending encoder output per subscription, and one media send in flight. If encoding or send work is occupied, newer unencoded work is dropped rather than queued. One actual encoded drop or undeliverable output forces a later IDR; replacing a not-yet-encoded raw frame does not break decoder references.

Every RVID send has a ten-second completion deadline. A newly accepted stream has 15 seconds to produce its first frame, and a running stream may not remain silent beyond 15 seconds. A peer that stops reading loses the stream instead of retaining unlimited memory. At the published maximum, two viewers can still consume roughly 3 Mbit/s plus protocol overhead, so data-plane separation prevents queue coupling but not shared-radio contention.

\section{Decode framing before decoding H.264}

On Vision Pro, the Network.framework adapter first reconstructs complete RVID frames from arbitrary receive chunks. It rejects invalid magic, version, reserved bytes, payload sizes, and ordering before decoding JSON or RBVD. The media decoder then checks session ID, subscription ID, codec, configuration generation, sequence, timing, AVCC length width, and size. These checks occur before creating Core Media state.

\texttt{H264SampleBufferFactory} sorts and extracts SPS/PPS, copies their bytes into temporary stable pointers, and calls \texttt{CMVideoFormatDescriptionCreateFromH264ParameterSets}. It verifies that coded dimensions remain within macroblock rounding of the negotiated size and that clean-aperture presentation dimensions match. This prevents hostile or corrupted parameter sets from expanding the decoder beyond the agreed geometry.

For each accepted access unit, the factory allocates a \texttt{CMBlockBuffer}, copies the bounded AVCC payload, creates timing, and asks Core Media to create a ready sample buffer. The not-sync attachment mirrors the key-frame flag. The display-immediately attachment requests low-latency presentation rather than building a long presentation queue.

\section{Present and recover without blocking control}

The actor-owned \texttt{H264VideoReceiver} feeds an \texttt{AVSampleBufferVideoRenderer} obtained from the UI's display layer. If the renderer is temporarily full, it waits only about 50 ms in short cancellable intervals. Continued pressure drops the access unit and begins recovery rather than accumulating frames.

Sequence gaps, missing configuration, generation mismatch, decoder failure, or \texttt{requiresFlushToResumeDecoding} make the validator require a key frame. Recovery flushes decoder presentation state, rate-limits key-frame feedback to no faster than 500 ms, and discards dependent frames until a valid key frame arrives. A configuration-generation change also flushes before accepting the new decoder state. Statistics report received, rendered, dropped, bytes, last sequence, and key-frame requests at a bounded update rate.

\section*{Timing, reliability, and tradeoffs}

Capture wall-clock time measures end-to-end age. Presentation timestamps provide an internal monotonic media timeline. Duration is derived from the negotiated frame rate. These clocks answer different questions and should not be substituted for one another.

Ordered reliable QUIC simplifies configuration and access-unit ordering, but packet loss can delay later video bytes through head-of-line blocking. Because control uses another QUIC connection, video loss does not queue a stop command, although both flows share Wi-Fi airtime. Future QUIC datagrams would trade retransmission for application-managed loss recovery and require a new negotiated contract; they are not silently enabled by the current enum.

\section{Servo and arm control}

Cerebro uses Maestro servo channels plus AMBER Python paths. ROB's present AMBER evidence describes two seven-joint B1 arms and separate grippers. The Ubuntu-side directories \texttt{L-10} and \texttt{R-11} bind \texttt{can10}/\texttt{can11}, UDP ports 26001/26002, and LCM prefixes \texttt{Left\_}/\texttt{Right\_}. Network clients send UDP or LCM to one core per side; each core, not the client, interfaces with its actuators over CAN.

The checked-in V2 protocol provides status, joint and Cartesian moves, calibration, gripper control, and mode control. Its packed UDP header contains a 16-bit command, 16-bit length, and 32-bit counter. The current client needs stronger validation of source, size, command, counter, freshness, finite values, and authentication before production use. Archived binaries do not include enough source to reconstruct their actuator-level CAN protocol.

URDF models provide link geometry, joint origins, axes, and limits, but they do not prove ROB's physical mount transforms, encoder zero, payload, safe stopping, or cable collision volume. Measure torso-to-left-base, torso-to-right-base, and each tool transform; then verify forward kinematics over several supported poses before inverse kinematics. Volume 6 contains the protocol tables, joint model, Ubuntu reproduction procedure, and staged dual-arm commissioning gates.

\section{Keyframe animation}

\Implemented\quad \texttt{KeyframeAnimationManager.swift} persists JSON values for arm channels, head/neck, tread movement, flipper, actuator, torso rotation, and speech. Keyframes are reusable data: a sequence can describe expressive behavior independently of the UI that recorded it.

\Commanded\quad Historical servo ``animation'' helpers in \texttt{ROBSerialBox.m} do not yet perform robust smooth interpolation; they update their targets immediately. New interpolation must respect joint rates, synchronization, limits, collisions, and cancellation.

\section{Speech}

\Implemented\quad Cerebro configures an Apple local speech-recognition path and uses local speech synthesis for completed responses. This path reduces network dependence, but it does not describe every microphone use: when the separately optional Gemini session is enabled, microphone audio may be streamed to an external cloud service. Both paths require microphone permissions, public notice, and a plan for unintended capture.

\section{Controller-activated autonomy}

\Experimental\quad The social-roam profile begins only after explicit controller authorization, captures an activation pose and radius, uses fresh filtered lidar scans, and limits tread speed. Near 82 percent of the configured radius it attempts to return toward its origin; this is not verified containment. Missing or stale lidar requests a motion stop and transitions to \texttt{waitingForLidar} while the autonomy session remains active. Manual takeover, explicit stop, expiry, replacement, or shutdown ends the session. Local deterministic code owns execution.

\section{Gemini integration}

\Experimental\quad The entire Gemini integration defaults off and requires explicit enablement plus credentials. Once enabled, the current configuration defaults camera-frame and microphone-audio streaming on unless each stream is separately disabled. The robot-action tool remains separately off by default. Its schema declares \texttt{look\_at}, \texttt{play\_gesture}, \texttt{request\_pick}, \texttt{navigate\_relative}, and \texttt{stop\_motion}; the model never receives arbitrary raw serial output. Only \texttt{stop\_motion} has an immediate local executor, and even that returns a partial result because arm hold is unverified. The other actions have no deterministic executor: the controller console records approval or rejection but does not actuate hardware.

\Planned\quad General picking is intentionally unavailable. Missing prerequisites include calibrated camera-to-arm transforms, complete neck mapping, joint feedback, inverse kinematics, collision checking, grasp planning, and a verified executor.

\section{Local MLX, comedy, and battle training}

\ROBSourceNow{Cerebro/Cerebro/ROBStageShowCoordinator.swift}{Read the strict show protocol and fixture tests first, then compare \texttt{ROBMLXImprovisationProvider.swift}, \texttt{ROBSaberChoreography.swift}, and the JSON catalog under \texttt{StageShows}.}

The stage-show coordinator is a cancellable state machine rather than a sequence of arbitrary shell or motor commands. Show JSON may request speech, waits, checkpoints, allow-listed gestures, and adaptive dialogue; it may not contain raw joints, hosts, ports, or shell commands. Dry Run has no side effects. Offline uses authored lines. Local mode may use a schema-validated MLX or loopback llama.cpp line. Adaptive mode may give a trusted local plan to Gemini, but timeout and failure fall back through validated local text to mandatory authored text. Late model completions are correlated and discarded.

The comedy show demonstrates why fallback belongs to creative design: roughly ten minutes of timing cannot depend on one network or model response. Progressive saber training maps named profiles to bounded Cartesian transforms and duration. It must remain theatrical, slow, guarded choreography with soft props, a participant boundary, dry-run visualization, independent stop, transform, inverse-kinematics, and collision validation, and no autonomous contact. Vision may score timing or offer spoken coaching; pixels do not directly command the AMBER arms.

\chapter{Test strategy and safety case}

\section{Requirements traceability}

Every test should trace to a requirement and hazard. A simple matrix includes requirement ID, hazard ID, source revision, test method, equipment, pass criteria, result, evidence path, reviewer, and date. ``It worked once'' is not a repeatable acceptance criterion.

\section{Failure injection}

Test the robot's response when:

\begin{itemize}
  \item joystick input stops or becomes non-finite;
  \item Watch-to-phone relay disappears;
  \item controller packets duplicate, reorder, age, or exceed bounds;
  \item control network disappears while video continues;
  \item video decoder stalls or requests a keyframe;
  \item USB serial is unplugged or filled with malformed bytes;
  \item Arduino boots with IMU missing;
  \item an IR sensor sticks low or produces grass reflections;
  \item lidar data becomes stale or claims an impossible timestamp;
  \item DepthAI helper exits or the camera disconnects;
  \item an actuator reaches a mechanical stop;
  \item one motor direction is reversed;
  \item a credential is revoked during active control;
  \item autonomy loses its sensor or leaves its radius;
  \item an AI tool proposes an unsupported or stale action;
  \item physical E-stop is pressed at each declared operating speed and pose.
\end{itemize}

\section{Measure stop behavior}

For each layer, measure detection time, command time, electrical response, mechanical response, and final state. Report surface, slope, load, battery state, speed, controller mode, firmware/software revision, sample rate, and uncertainty. ``600 ms watchdog'' is not the same as ``stopped within 600 ms.''

\begin{safetybox}
Use rated stands, restraints, remote observation, current limiting, a spotter, and an independent stop before intentionally injecting failures. Some failures should be tested only in simulation or on surrogate hardware.
\end{safetybox}

\chapter{Present ROB safely in public}

\section{Roles}

Assign at least these responsibilities:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Lead operator}: owns arm/disarm and the primary controller.
  \item \textbf{Safety spotter}: watches the robot and crowd, keeps a hand near the physical E-stop, and may stop without asking.
  \item \textbf{Educator}: explains ROB without distracting the operator.
  \item \textbf{Technical support}: monitors power, temperature, logs, and recovery.
  \item \textbf{Event liaison}: coordinates space, charging, barriers, accessibility, and emergency requirements.
\end{itemize}

One person may fill multiple roles only if workload and risk allow it. Never make the operator answer a long technical question while ROB moves near visitors.

\section{Pre-show checklist}

\begin{itemize}[label={\protect\raisebox{0.08em}{\protect\fbox{\protect\rule{0pt}{0.55em}\protect\rule{0.55em}{0pt}}}}]
  \item current approved software/firmware versions recorded;
  \item batteries inspected, charged under the approved procedure, and restrained;
  \item fuses, disconnect, and E-stop visually inspected and function-tested;
  \item treads, chain, sprockets, guards, shafts, collars, mounts, fasteners, and torque marks inspected;
  \item arm, neck, flipper, and actuator limits checked in a protected area;
  \item controllers uniquely paired; unused credentials revoked;
  \item control and optional video links tested under venue network conditions;
  \item speed cap and operating mode verified on every controller;
  \item barrier, route, slope, floor transitions, cables, exits, and charging location inspected;
  \item camera, microphone, recording, location, and cloud-AI policy displayed;
  \item fire response, first aid, lift/transport, and fault-recovery plans briefed;
  \item spare tools and parts controlled so visitors cannot reach them;
  \item dry rehearsal completed with the full team.
\end{itemize}

\section{Show-state model}

Use explicit visible states: Transport Safe, Display Only, Demo Armed, Moving, Stopped, Faulted, Charging, and Maintenance. Each transition names who authorizes it and what checklist applies. Reconnecting a controller or restarting software never silently arms motion.

\section{Audience and privacy}

Keep an accessible viewing route. Do not require visitors to step over cables. Mark pinch and tread boundaries clearly. Avoid publishing faces without consent. When camera, microphone, recording, or cloud processing is active, use plain-language signage and a visible indicator; provide a quick disable path and a non-recorded way to participate.

\chapter{Keep the robot understandable}

\section{Before and after every operating day}

Inspect for loose fasteners, moved torque marks, chain and tread damage, shaft-collar movement, bracket cracks or bending, wire abrasion, connector heating, battery swelling or damage, fluid or debris, blocked ventilation, actuator looseness, unusual sound, camera security, E-stop function, and log errors.

After operation, record runtime, surfaces, mode, maximum observed current and temperature if available, incidents, unexpected stops, repairs, and deferred defects. Quarantine the robot from powered use when a red condition remains unresolved.

\section{Scheduled maintenance record}

For every interval, name the component, task, acceptable condition, tool, replacement part, torque or measurement, evidence, and sign-off. Intervals may be hours, cycles, calendar time, or before each public show.

\section{Configuration control}

A change to code, pin mapping, motor polarity, brake logic, network framing, controller speed cap, arm limit, battery arrangement, or bracket can invalidate earlier tests. Give hardware, firmware, software, calibration, wiring, and operating procedure separate revision identifiers, then record the tested combination.
