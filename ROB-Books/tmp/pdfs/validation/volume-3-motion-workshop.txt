A safe tour from force and torque to prototypes, measurements, brackets, chains, and tests.

ROB is heavy enough that small mechanical choices become serious. A shaft that is slightly out of line can
pull a chain sideways. A cable near a sprocket can become a hazard. A bracket that looks stiff may bend
when a long arm multiplies force.
The activities in this book use paper, cardboard, rulers, craft fasteners, and small educational kits. Milling,
drilling metal, welding, grinding, large batteries, chains, treads, flippers, and full-size actuators are red-
zone work for trained adults.

   HOW TO USE THIS BOOK

   Treat every photograph as evidence of one moment in an evolving prototype. Look for loads, motion
   paths, fasteners, alignment, access, and risk. Do not copy dimensions from perspective photographs.

   ONE CURRENT ARDUINO, THREE HISTORICAL SKETCHES

   The ROBArduino archive preserves Base, Torso, and Head sketches from an earlier three-Arduino de-
   sign. The builder reports that present-day ROB uses only the Base sketch. This book therefore treats
   Base behavior as the current low-level firmware reference and labels Torso and Head behavior as re-
   tired historical evidence. Source files cannot prove which binary is flashed today; record the board
   identity, firmware hash, build environment, and upload date before operation.

   TWO AMBER B1 ARMS, ONE DOCUMENTED CONTROL CHAIN

   ROB carries two seven-joint AMBER B1 arms. The repository snapshot separates their Ubuntu-side
   cores as L-10 and R-11: network clients use UDP or LCM, each core binds a stable CAN interface, and
   CAN carries commands and feedback between that core and its arm actuators. The AmberHomeFolder
   tree is evidence from one Ubuntu machine, not a deployable image; never copy its credentials, logs,
   virtual environment, or machine-specific identifiers. Volume 6 documents the inspected protocol,
   URDF models, reproducible setup, verification gates, and unresolved configuration differences.

What must the base survive?
A good drawing begins with questions about forces and uses. ROB’s base must support its own structure,
batteries, computers, torso, head, arms, and anything the arms carry. It must also react to bumps, turns,
starts, stops, flipper motion, transport, and curious crowds.
Engineers name several kinds of load:

• compression: parts push together;
• tension: parts pull apart;
• shear: layers try to slide past one another;
• bending: one side stretches while the other compresses;
• torsion: a part twists about its length;
• impact: force changes quickly during a collision or drop.

Moment: force with a lever arm
A simple bending moment is M = F d, where F is force and d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot.
The same payload creates more moment when the arm extends farther.

   RULER LEVER TEST

   Hold a ruler at one end. Place one coin 2 cm from your hand, then move it to 20 cm. The coin’s weight
   did not change, but your wrist feels a larger moment. Draw the pivot, force arrow, and distance. Do
   not use ROB’s powered arm for this experiment.

  SOURCE TRAIL --- ANALYZING NOW

  File: ROBArduino/ROBOT_CEREBELLULAR_BASE_APP/ROBOT_CEREBELLULAR_BASE_APP.ino
  Why it is here: The Base sketch supplies the actual left/right tread command behavior discussed
  here; the mechanical photographs explain what those outputs must move.

ROB steers by commanding the left and right treads differently. This is differential drive. It is mechanically
simple because there is no steering linkage, but turning a long tread on high-friction ground can demand
a great deal of torque. Parts of the tread must slide sideways as the robot rotates.
The purchased SuperDroid Robots LT2 tread system was ROB’s one non-negotiable starting interface.
The builder first mocked its envelope in cardboard, then planned maple-plywood parts around it and a
flipper/third-wheel variation inspired by Johnny 5. That is systems engineering in a wonderfully practical
form: freeze the one part you cannot change, represent it cheaply, and iterate everything that touches it.
The original LT2 is designed as a skid-steer tracked platform. SuperDroid’s own support material warns
through its payload discussion that added weight and high-traction treads make the sideways skid re-
quired for turning more difficult. ROB added arms, cameras, computers, an inverter, batteries, and struc-
ture far above the base. On concrete, the builder now often turns with small forward/back arcs like a car
instead of demanding a stationary pivot. This is not merely a software inconvenience; it is the floor, tread
contact patch, normal force, center of mass, gear ratio, motor current limit, and structure negotiating at
once.
That explains an idea in the 2021-2022 presentation: a small wheel, mounted perpendicular to the treads
and deployed by a linear actuator, could lift part of the robot so it pivots with less scrub. The slides call
this the ThirdWheel concept. The archive does not establish the final geometry or whether the concept
was completed.

   Computer commands and mechanical behavior meet at the floor. A program can request equal

Trade speed for torque
MISSION 3


Motors, gearing, and
sprockets
The motor label visible in ROB’s build photographs identifies a HengDrive B5685G family. The builder
recalls an early 12 V, roughly 80 rpm and 30 W arrangement, but the public evidence does not establish
the installed winding, gearbox, controller interface, or present rating on each axis. Preserve those values
as recollection until label photographs, non-destructive measurements, bounded tests, and an authorized
public manufacturer record resolve the configuration.
The builder tried 16-, 32-, 40-, and 45-tooth sprockets while searching for a useful balance. A tooth count
has no meaning by itself: the log must say whether it was the driver or driven sprocket and name its mate.
Larger speed at the tread can reduce available output torque, while a reduction chosen for strong flipper
lift can be painfully slow. ROB needed both: enough travel speed for a public robot and enough flipper
torque to raise part of the heavy droid so the tracks could undulate over stairs.


Gear ratio
If a small gear with 10 teeth drives a large gear with 40 teeth, the ideal speed ratio is 4:1. The output turns
one-quarter as fast and can deliver roughly four times the torque before losses.

                                                               driven teeth   40

Real gear trains lose energy through friction, deformation, bearing drag, and imperfect alignment. Chains
add flexibility in placement but need correct sprockets, tension, lubrication policy, guarding, and clear-

   GEAR CARD LAB

   Draw a 12-tooth driver and a 36-tooth driven gear. Predict output turns for three input turns. Reverse
   which gear drives and predict again. Label which setup favors speed and which favors torque.



What the 24 V boost changed
The builder later installed a large 12-to-24 V boost converter and recalls moving the motor supply to-
ward a 24 V bus. ROB immediately felt faster and stronger. The careful lesson is not “double voltage
always doubles torque.” For a given motor, back-EMF and voltage strongly affect available speed, while
torque follows current; the controller, converter, winding, gearing, battery sag, temperature, and load
limit the result. The exact installed motor/controller option, commanded voltage, measured bus voltage,
and converter margin are not established, so this observation remains history rather than an electrical
specification.

   BUILD A GEAR-RATIO TEST MATRIX

   Make rows for 16, 32, 40, and 45 teeth. Add columns for driver teeth, driven teeth, calculated ratio,
   unloaded tread speed, current during a gentle turn, flipper lift success, motor temperature, and notes
   about surface. Leave a cell blank when it was not measured. The best ratio is the one that satisfies
   the declared mission with margin, not the one with the most dramatic single number.

   THE UNDERPOWERED-MOTOR LESSON

   The visible label identifies a HengDrive B5685G-family motor, while the author recalls an early
   roughly 30 W, 12 V choice that was too slow and underpowered for the growing robot. Sprocket
   changes recovered torque but did not deliver the intended speed; a later 12-to-24 V boost stage
   made ROB stronger and faster while it remained slow and high-torque. In hindsight the author would
   begin a redesign around at least roughly 120 W per drive motor and about four times the speed, but
   that is a design goal, not a drop-in specification. Exact winding, gearbox, controller logic, installed
   sprockets, current, temperature, and lift margin remain unmeasured. Private manufacturer drawings
   are excluded from the public edition.

Alignment is a three-dimensional problem
A chain may look aligned from the side while being offset toward or away from the camera. A shaft can
be parallel in one view but tilted in another. Good inspection uses multiple references:

• center distance between shafts;
• shaft parallelism;
• sprocket coplanarity;
• axial retention and shaft collars;
• chain slack or measured tension;
• clearance through the full suspension or frame motion;
• guards that keep cables, clothing, and fingers away.

A linear actuator often turns a motor internally, reduces speed through gears, and converts rotation into

MISSION 6


From measurement to
machined part
A safe fabrication plan separates design decisions from machine operation.

1. Capture requirements. What loads, interfaces, clearances, and service access matter?
2. Measure. Record multiple datums, units, tool, and uncertainty.
3. Model or template. Create a drawing that another person can inspect.
4. Review. Check motion envelopes, fastener access, cable paths, and hazards.
5. Plan operations. Choose stock, workholding, tool reach, sequence, and inspection points.
6. Make under supervision. Follow the shop’s training and operating rules.
7. Deburr, clean, and inspect. Chips and sharp edges do not belong near wiring.
8. Fit without power. Check alignment through the full motion envelope.
9. Test progressively. Begin unloaded and isolated, then add one risk at a time.

 MISSION 7


 Make it buildable and
 repairable
A robot is assembled in time, not all at once. If one bolt becomes unreachable after a battery is installed,
the sequence matters. If a cable must pass through a plate before a connector is attached, the sequence
matters. If a tread blocks the cover that protects a fuse, service time and safety are affected.


 A proposed base sequence to verify
This is a planning scaffold, not ROB’s confirmed build order:

 1. inspect the bare chassis and all threaded features;
 2. install guarded mechanical pivots, bearings, and fixed brackets;
 3. trial-fit motors, sprockets, shafts, and chains without power;
 4. verify rotation by hand and measure alignment;
 5. add power hardware, protection, and labeled harnesses;
 6. install batteries last among heavy internal parts, using a safe lift plan;
 7. complete continuity, polarity, insulation, and protective-device checks;
 8. raise treads on rated stands and perform bounded low-power direction tests;
 9. reinstall guards and covers before ground testing;
10. record torque marks, photos, measurements, and test results.

DEEP LAB


Forces leave clues
MISSION 9


Draw the forces before
choosing a motor
Motion begins with interactions. Gravity pulls mass toward Earth. A surface pushes back with a support
force. Friction can provide useful traction or resist a skid turn. Motors create torque, but the ground
supplies the external force that accelerates a tracked robot. A free-body diagram isolates one object and
draws each external force once.


Mass, weight, force, and torque
Mass describes inertia; weight is gravitational force on that mass. Force changes linear motion. Torque
changes rotational motion. The same force produces more torque when applied farther from an axis:
τ = F r⊥ . Gears trade angular speed for torque ideally, while real transmissions lose energy to friction,
flex, impact, and misalignment.

  CARDBOARD LEVER LAB

  Balance a ruler on a rounded pencil and use identical erasers as loads. Predict how many erasers
  at two units from the pivot balance one eraser at four units. Test gently with hands clear. Record
  distance and count. Explain why equal torque can occur with unequal force.



Why tracked turning costs energy
In a differential turn, left and right tread speeds set the path. Equal forward speeds approximate a straight
line. Opposite speeds create a pivot turn. But a long tread contact patch cannot roll sideways like a
steering wheel; parts of it scrub across the floor. Surface, tread material, normal load, geometry, and
debris change the required torque and heat.

  A MODEL HAS A DOMAIN
  The simple differential-drive model treats each side like one ideal wheel and predicts geometry well
  on some surfaces. It does not automatically predict tread deformation, slip, chain shock, motor heat-
  ing, or carpet bunching. State what a model includes, what it ignores, and where evidence says it is
  useful.

  DESIGN A FAIR RAMP TEST ON PAPER

  Choose a small classroom toy—not ROB—and plan a ramp investigation. Define the ramp surface,

Error budgets make assemblies possible
Every cut, hole, shaft, bracket, bearing, and spacer varies. A tolerance says how much variation is accept-
able. A tolerance stack asks how several variations combine at an interface. Datums tell every maker
where measurements begin. Inspection then compares the built feature with the drawing rather than with
memory.

  DESIGN FOR FAILURE AND SERVICE

  Ask where stored energy goes if a chain breaks, a fastener loosens, a bearing seizes, or a cable
  snags. Then ask whether the failed part can be inspected and replaced without dismantling unre-
  lated systems. Guards, secondary retention, accessible connectors, witness marks, and documented
  assembly order are design features, not cleanup work.
